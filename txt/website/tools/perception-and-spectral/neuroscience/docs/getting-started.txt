OPEN THE TOOL
=============

Go to
https://auricartisan.com/tool/general/perception-and-neuroscience/neuroscience/.
The page opens on the Lab tab with a default stimulus already drawn: a
chromatic grating based on the colour `#3A8CFF`, at 4.0 cycles per degree and
25% contrast, viewed from 57 cm on a screen with 0.248 mm pixels.

A note strip above the tabs says what the pictures rest on: the L and M cones
peak 25 nm apart in the published data, but the cone table this page draws
peaks them at the same wavelength. The strip cannot be closed. Its Data link
opens the Data tab, where the reason is explained.

THE SIX TABS
============

  Tab:            Lab
  What it is for: The workbench: controls on the left, the stimulus and the
                  chosen stage map on the right, the cone figure and the
                  readouts below

  Tab:            Pipeline
  What it is for: Where a colour goes before any cortex sees it, step by step,
                  with the opponent values of seven test colours

  Tab:            Cells
  What it is for: All the stages side by side over the same small stimulus

  Tab:            Data
  What it is for: The register of every dataset and transform the tool rests
                  on, with a status for each; you can install a cone table
                  here

  Tab:            Export
  What it is for: A list of colours through the front end, and the download,
                  copy and link buttons

  Tab:            Reference
  What it is for: Standards, formulas, citations and research notes, with a
                  search box

A TOUR OF THE LAB TAB
=====================

The rail (left). From top to bottom: Stimulus, Geometry, the frequency,
contrast and pupil sliders, Stages, Show, then the settings of the stages that
are switched on (Retina, V1, V4 preferred direction) and Colour vision. Each
group has a short hint under it. Sliders show their current value beside their
label.

The stage (right).

  - Two square canvases, 256 × 256 pixels each: Stimulus on the left and the
    selected map on the right, named under it (for example Retina, on-centre).
    The header gives the size and the angle one pixel covers at your stated
    geometry.
  - The scale bar: the map's minimum on the left, the colour ramp, the maximum
    on the right, and what the numbers are.
  - The front end: where the two cone curves actually sit — a figure of the L,
    M and S curves with dashed lines at the published peak wavelengths.
  - Two rows of readouts: L − M peak gap, Published gap, Neutral off-axis,
    Contrast achieved, then Sensitivity at this frequency, The curve's peak,
    Frame RMS contrast and Nyquist limit of the grid.
  - Notes about what the page does not report, and two buttons: Save the map
    and Copy a link to this view.

Warnings appear between these parts only when needed: one when the frequency
is above your screen's Nyquist limit, one when the contrast you asked for does
not fit in sRGB.

YOUR FIRST WALK THROUGH THE STAGES
==================================

  1. Set Viewing distance and Pixel pitch for your own screen. For a pixel
     pitch, divide the screen's visible width in millimetres by its horizontal
     resolution. The header above the canvases updates with the degrees per
     pixel, and Nyquist limit of the grid shows the finest frequency your
     screen can draw.
  2. Leave Spatial frequency at 4.0 c/deg and Contrast at 25%.
  3. In Show, pick Retina, on-centre. The map shows where the centre-surround
     cells respond. Read the numbers at the ends of the scale bar: every map
     is stretched to its own range, so those numbers are what the colours
     mean.
  4. Pick Retina, off-centre. With the default chromatic grating this map can
     come out flat, and the scale bar then says flat — this stage found no
     variation in this stimulus. That is a result, not a fault: see Explore
     the five stages [explore-the-stages.txt].
  5. Pick LGN, L − M and LGN, S − (L+M)/2. These are the red-green and
     blue-yellow opponent channels, and the grating drives them strongly.
  6. Pick V1, complex-cell energy, then V1, preferred orientation. The second
     is drawn as a colour wheel: hue is the preferred orientation from 0° to
     180°, and paler pixels are less selective.
  7. Change Carrier to Luminance. The grating is now grey stripes, lighter and
     darker than a grey with the base colour's luminance. The chromatic LGN
     channels flatten, and the retinal and V1 maps respond to the light-dark
     swing.
  8. Double-click either canvas to see it enlarged. Press `Esc` to close.

You should now see the stimulus on the left and a map on the right that
changes with every control you move.
