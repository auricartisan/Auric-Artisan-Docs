Interpolation and gamut

How the four colour spaces blend two colours differently, what short and long hue routes and easing do, how evenness is measured, and how out-of-gamut steps are brought back.

A ramp is made by interpolation: choosing colours part of the way between the two ends. What "part of the way" means depends on the colour space the blending happens in, so the same two colours give different ramps in different spaces.

The four spaces

SpaceWhat is blendedCharacter of the ramp
sRGBThe red, green and blue values as stored in a web colourThe browser's default. Middles between very different colours can turn dull or dark
Linear RGBRed, green and blue as amounts of light, before the sRGB curve is appliedPhysically like mixing light. Middles tend to look too light, and the dark end changes quickly
HSLHue, saturation and lightness, a cylinder built from sRGBKeeps colours saturated, but HSL lightness is not perceived lightness, so brightness can bulge in the middle
OKLCHLightness, chroma and hue in OKLabDesigned so equal steps look equal. Keeps colourfulness through the middle. The default

OKLab and OKLCH

OKLab is a perceptual colour space published by Björn Ottosson in 2020. It has a lightness axis, L, and two colour axes, a and b, arranged so that equal distances look roughly equally different. OKLCH is the same space in polar form: L for lightness, C for chroma (how colourful, the distance from grey), and H for hue (an angle, 0° to 360°). Blending lightness, chroma and hue separately gives ramps whose steps look even and whose middle stays colourful.

sRGB and linear RGB

sRGB values are encoded with a curve that spends more of the 0 to 255 range on dark tones, roughly matching how eyes see. Blending the encoded values is quick and familiar; it is what CSS gradients do by default. Linear RGB undoes that curve first, so the blend is proportional to light. Neither is designed to make equal steps look equal.

HSL

HSL rearranges sRGB into hue, saturation and lightness. It is easy to reason about, but its lightness is a simple average of the largest and smallest channel, not a perceptual measure: fully saturated yellow and fully saturated blue have the same HSL lightness and look very different.

Short and long hue routes

In the two polar spaces, OKLCH and HSL, hue is an angle, and there are two ways round the circle from one hue to another.

  • Short hue takes the smaller arc, never more than 180°.
  • Long hue goes the other way round, through the hues the short route skips.

This is a choice of route, not a different space, which is why the tool offers four spaces but six routes. The long route covers much more distance when the two hues are close together; when they are nearly opposite, the two routes are similar in length.

sRGB and linear RGB have no hue angle, so they have one route each.

Easing

Easing decides where along the way each step lands. The tool uses the familiar CSS timing curves:

EasingWhere the steps land
linearEvenly spaced along the route
ease-inBunched near the start, spreading out towards the end
ease-outSpread out at the start, bunched near the end
ease-in-outBunched at both ends, spread in the middle
stepOnly five distinct positions (0, ¼, ½, ¾ and the end), so neighbouring steps repeat colours

Easing changes where the steps land, not the path the ramp takes. That is why it barely changes the average difference between steps but can change how even they are a great deal.

Measuring a ramp

  • ΔE₀₀ is the CIEDE2000 colour difference, the CIE's standard formula for how different two colours look. The tool measures it between each step and the next.
  • mean ΔE₀₀ is the average of those differences: how far apart the steps are.
  • evenness is the standard deviation of those differences: how much they vary. Zero would mean every step is exactly as far from the next. Lower is more even.
  • largest jump is the biggest single difference, and where it is.

The mean and the evenness answer different questions and move independently. A ramp is usually being asked the evenness question.

Gamut and gamut mapping

A gamut is the range of colours a device or colour space can show. sRGB, the gamut of ordinary screens and web colours, cannot hold every colour OKLCH can describe. Blending in OKLCH between two saturated colours often asks for a middle step more colourful than sRGB allows.

There are two ways to bring such a colour back:

  • Clipping each channel to 0 to 255 is simple, but it changes the hue and lightness as well as the colourfulness.
  • Gamut mapping by chroma, which this tool uses, keeps the lightness and hue you asked for and reduces only the chroma, until the colour just fits. The result sits on the edge of sRGB at the right lightness and hue.

The tool finds the edge by halving the search twenty times, which is far finer than one step of an 8-bit channel. Steps that were brought back this way are marked with a gold underline in the ramp and outlined in amber on the hue circle, and moved to fit sRGB counts them.

Only the OKLCH routes can leave sRGB. Blends in sRGB, linear RGB and HSL always stay inside it.

The desaturated middle

A ramp between two colours on opposite sides of the colour wheel, blended in sRGB or linear RGB, passes close to grey in the middle, because the straight line between them crosses the neutral axis. Blending in OKLCH goes round the hue circle instead and stays colourful. The Spaces tab draws the same two ends in sRGB, Linear RGB and OKLCH, short hue and gives the chroma of the least colourful step of each, measured in OKLCH, so the three can be compared on one scale.
