Read a colour

Everything on The colour tab: the contrast verdict, the colour-blindness values, every notation and metric, and the nearest neighbours.

The colour tab shows the full record of one colour. It puts the verdict first — what the colour can carry as text — then the evidence, then the raw numbers.

Open a colour

Any of these opens a colour on The colour tab:

  • select a tile in the grid, or focus it and press Enter or Space;
  • select one of the five neighbour cells under Start from a colour you already have;
  • select Open a random colour;
  • select a tile on the Saved tab.

The tab switches straight away; the full record loads a moment later. If you open the tab before choosing a colour, it says No colour open with the hint Pick one from the atlas, or paste a hex above to find its neighbours.

The identity block

At the top left:

  • a large swatch of the colour;
  • its hex code in capitals, for example #76CDF6;
  • a line with Atlas id and the id number, followed by the colour's labels (emotion, art movement, mood, design usage and design tag), separated by dots.

The block also shows Copy and Save buttons. To copy values or save the colour, use the Export tab or the star on the colour's tile; see Troubleshooting if these two buttons do not respond.

What it can carry

Two specimens show the sentence "The quick brown fox jumps over the lazy dog." written in the colour:

  • on a white background, with the contrast ratio and the words on white;
  • on a black background, with the ratio and on black.

Ratios of 4.5:1 or more are marked as passing, lower ratios as failing.

Under the specimens, one sentence sums the colour up:

SituationSentence
4.5:1 or more on bothRare: it carries body text on either ground.
4.5:1 or more on white onlyA dark-on-light colour. Use it for text on a light page; on black it disappears.
4.5:1 or more on black onlyA light-on-dark colour. It is not a body-text colour on a light page and no weight will make it one — but on black it is one of the strongest in the atlas.
Below 4.5:1 on bothIt carries body text on neither white nor black. This is a colour to draw with, not to write in.

Under other eyes

Four chips show the colour as it appears to different kinds of colour vision:

ChipNoteMeaning
Normal visionthe colour as storedThe colour itself.
Protanopiared-blindNo working red-sensitive cones.
Deuteranopiagreen-blindNo working green-sensitive cones.
Tritanopiablue-blindNo working blue-sensitive cones.

Each chip shows its hex code. As the page says, "The atlas stores the simulated colour, not a filter over the swatch — so these are values you can copy." You can use these hex codes to check whether two colours in a design stay distinct for colour-blind readers.

Every space, and the metrics

A table of the colour's values:

RowWhat it shows
RGBRed, green and blue, each 0 to 255.
HSLHue in degrees, saturation and lightness as percentages.
CIE L*a*b*Lightness L (0 to 100) and the a (green to red) and b (blue to yellow) axes.
OKLabThe same idea in the newer OKLab space: L, a and b.
OKLCHOKLab as lightness L, chroma C and hue h in degrees.
CMYKCyan, magenta, yellow and black, as percentages.
Relative luminanceHow much light the colour gives off, from 0 (black) to 1 (white). Contrast ratios are built from this.
ChromaColourfulness, as OKLCH chroma.
VividnessA precomputed measure of how vivid the colour is.
Perceptual uniformityA precomputed score stored with the colour.

Values are shown to two, four or more decimal places. A dash means the value is not in the record.

Nearest in the atlas

The last block shows the five atlas colours closest to this one, each with its hex code and its distance, for example ΔE 2.10 — indistinguishable. The distance words mean the same as in Find a colour's nearest neighbours.

Result

You know whether the colour can carry body text on white or black, what it becomes for red-, green- and blue-blind readers, its value in every notation, and which catalogued colours are near it.
